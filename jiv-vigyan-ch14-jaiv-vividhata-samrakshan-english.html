📖 🌟 Do you know?
In 1973, when India's tiger population had dwindled to just 1,827, then-Prime Minister Indira Gandhi took a bold decision—launching "Project Tiger," under which nine sanctuaries across the country, including Ranthambore, were dedicated to tiger conservation. At the time, few believed this effort would succeed. Today, five decades later, India's tiger population has risen to 3,682 (2022 census)—nearly 80% of the world's tigers are found in India! Rajasthan's Sariska Sanctuary represents the most dramatic chapter in this story—tigers were completely wiped out in 2004-05 (not a single tiger remained). Yet, thanks to a restoration program begun in 2008, 49 tigers were roaring back in Sariska by June 2025. This story teaches that biodiversity loss is not irreversible if we take timely, scientific conservation efforts. Let us understand this important topic of biodiversity, natural resource conservation and sustainable development in detail.

1 Introduction to Biodiversity – Definition and Levels

Biodiversity (short for biological diversity) refers to the variety of all living organisms—plants, animals, and microorganisms—on Earth, and the complex relationships among them and their environments. The term was first proposed by Walter G. Rosen in 1985, and became a central theme of global policy following the signing of the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD) at the 1992 Rio Earth Summit.

Three levels of biodiversity

levelDescription and examples
Genetic DiversityGenetic variation among individuals within a species; examples: thousands of indigenous varieties of rice, different blood groups in humans
Species DiversityThe number and diversity of different species found in an area; for example, India has approximately 100,000 animal species and 50,000 plant species.
Ecosystem DiversityThe variety of ecosystems found in a region (see Chapter 13); examples: forests, wetlands, deserts, mountains
📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 💡 All three levels together in Rajasthan

All three levels of biodiversity are clearly visible in Rajasthan—at the genetic level, local animal breeds (such as the Marwari horse, Tharparkar cow, Marwari goat) and crop varieties (such as indigenous varieties of millet and fennel—see Chapter 10); at the species level, tiger, chinkara, great Indian bustard (state bird) and hundreds of other species; and at the ecosystem level, diverse ecosystems such as the Thar Desert, Aravalli forests, Keoladeo wetlands.

2 Importance of biodiversity – ecological, economic and ethical values

Biodiversity is often called Earth's 'life-support system'—it not only stabilizes ecosystems but is also essential for the survival of human civilization. Its importance can be understood in three broad categories.

(A) Ecological/Environmental importance

  • Ecosystem Services: Pollination, air purification, water purification, soil formation, climate regulation—all these services depend on biodiversity.
  • Ecosystem stability: A system with greater biodiversity is more resilient to environmental shocks (e.g., disease outbreaks, climate change)—the more complex the food web, the more stable it is (see Chapter 13).

(B) Economic importance

  • Agriculture and food security: About 75% of the world's food crops depend on pollinators (bees, butterflies); the genetic diversity of wild crop relatives is invaluable for developing future disease-resistant varieties.
  • Medicine: About 50% of the world's modern medicines are based on compounds derived from natural sources (plants, microorganisms) — such as penicillin (fungus), taxol (yew tree, anti-cancer).
  • Ecotourism: Sanctuaries like Ranthambore, Sariska contribute significantly to the economy of Rajasthan through tourism, generating local employment.

(C) ethical and cultural significance

Many moral philosophers argue that every species has an 'intrinsic right' to survive, whether or not it provides direct economic benefit to humans. Indian culture also has a deep tradition of biodiversity conservation—sacred forests (such as the Orans of Rajasthan, see Chapter 13), the Bishnoi community's dedication to tree and wildlife conservation (Amrita Devi Bishnoi's sacrifice in 1730 to protect Khejri trees—the historical inspiration for the Chipko movement) are prime examples.

3 Biodiversity of India - Biodiversity Hotspots

India is one of the world's 17 "mega-diverse" countries, containing approximately 7-8% of the world's known species in just 2.4% of the world's land area. Over 100,000 animal species and over 50,000 plant species have been recorded in India.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण

🖼️ Western Ghats — one of India's four biodiversity hotspots, home to numerous endemic species

Four Biodiversity Hotspots of India

Biodiversity hotspots are areas that have exceptionally high species diversity and endemism, but also face severe anthropogenic threats – a concept proposed by British ecologist Norman Myers in 1988.

  • Western Ghats and Sri Lanka: Widespread across Kerala, Karnataka, Tamil Nadu, and Maharashtra; many endemic amphibian and plant species.
  • Himalaya: Eastern and Western Himalayas; home to rare species like snow leopard and musk deer.
  • Indo-Burma: including northeastern India; Extremely rich and little-studied area.
  • Sundaland: Nicobar Islands is its Indian part.

4 Biodiversity of Rajasthan – Flora and Fauna

Rajasthan, the largest state of India, due to its diverse geographical features—Thar Desert, Aravalli mountain range, southern moist forest region (Vagad and Mewar), eastern plains—supports a highly diverse flora and fauna, adapted to a uniquely arid and semi-arid ecosystem.

Major vegetation of Rajasthan

Khejri (Prosopis cineraria, the state tree of Rajasthan) is the most important vegetation of the desert region—it is called the 'Kalpavriksha of the Desert' because it provides fodder, fuelwood, and shade to animals, and its deep roots prevent soil erosion. Other important vegetation includes the Rohida (Tecomella undulata, the state flowering tree of Rajasthan), acacia, kair, ber and savan grass, which show special adaptations to desert and semi-arid conditions, unlike the teak and salar forests of southern Rajasthan.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण

Urgency of Great Indian Bustard Conservation:The Great Indian Bustard (Great Indian Bustard) is one of the world's heaviest flying birds, with a global population of just around 150 remaining, most of which are found in the Desert National Park in the Jaisalmer region of Rajasthan. Collisions with high-altitude transmission power lines are a major cause of mortality—hence the 'Great Indian Bustard Conservation Project', which involves undergrounding power lines and installing bird diverters in critical areas.

Rajasthan plays a leading role in biodiversity conservation through its network of protected areas, which include national parks, wildlife sanctuaries and tiger reserves.

💡 Sariska — One of the world's most remarkable tiger recovery stories: Tigers completely disappeared from Sariska in 2004-05 due to poaching—one of the biggest setbacks in India's wildlife conservation history. However, since 2008, a reintroduction program has been launched in Sariska, led by the National Tiger Conservation Authority (NTCA), by translocating tigers from Ranthambore. In less than two decades, Sariska has 49 tigers by June 2025—proving that even severe biodiversity loss can be reversed with scientific planning and political will.

  • Sajjangarh Wildlife Sanctuary (Udaipur), Jaisamand Wildlife Sanctuary, Talchhapar Blackbuck Sanctuary (Churu): Other important protected areas of the state.

6 Threats to Biodiversity – Causes of Extinction

According to scientists, Earth is currently experiencing the sixth major mass extinction in its history—but unlike the first five (such as the extinction of the dinosaurs), this current one is primarily caused by anthropogenic factors.

cause of dangerBrief summary
Habitat LossDeforestation, urbanization, agricultural expansion — the biggest causes of extinction
OverexploitationPoaching, overfishing, uncontrolled collection of forest products
pollutionAir, water and soil pollution (see Chapter 13) poisoning the habitat of species
Climate changeChanging temperature and rainfall patterns making the species' natural habitat unsuitable
Invasive Speciesintroduction of alien species that compete for resources with native species
Pressure on climate-adapted speciesGreat Indian Bustard in Rajasthan – threatened by power lines, habitat loss and overgrazing
📌 महत्वपूर्ण — Sixth mass extinction

Scientific estimates suggest that the current species extinction rate is 100 to 1,000 times higher than the pre-human natural rate. This is also called the Holocene extinction or Anthropocene extinction, because it is primarily caused by human activities, not any natural geological event.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 🔑 Keywords

7 IUCN Red List and Threatened Species

The International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) produces the world's most comprehensive and scientifically authoritative biodiversity conservation status list, called the IUCN Red List – which scientifically assesses the extinction risk of species and classifies them into various categories.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण

🖼️ IUCN Red List categories — Extinct (EX) to Least Concern (LC), with Threatened categories CR, EN, VU in between.

Major categories

  • Extinct (EX): A species with no living members remaining; example: the dodo bird.
  • Critically Endangered (CR): An extremely high risk of extinction in the near future; example: Great Indian Bustard.
  • Endangered (EN): High risk of extinction; example: Bengal tiger (still in this category despite improvements in recent years).
  • Vulnerable (VU): at risk of extinction in the medium term; example: Indian elephant.
  • Near Threatened (Near Threatened — NT) and Least Concern (Least Concern — LC): Species with relatively stable populations.
📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 💡 Importance of IUCN Red List in India

The IUCN Red List is not just a scientific document, but an important basis for policy-making—India's Wildlife Protection Act 1972 (see next topic) and national conservation priorities are often set in accordance with IUCN classifications. When a species is declared 'critically endangered' (such as the Great Indian Bustard), it is a scientific signal of the need for urgent and special conservation intervention.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 🔑 Keywords

◆ IUCN Red List ◆ Extinct (EX) ◆ Critically Endangered (CR) ◆ Endangered (EN) ◆ Vulnerable (VU) ◆ Great Indian Bustard CR Category

8 Methods of Conservation - In-situ Conservation

In-situ conservation means conserving a species in its natural habitat—that is, 'protecting the species where it naturally lives.' This is considered the most effective method of conservation because it protects the species along with its entire ecosystem—its predators, prey, symbiotic relationships—rather than just as an isolated individual.

Self-defense typeSpeciality
National ParkHighest level of protection; human activity (grazing, forestry) is strictly prohibited; example: Ranthambore National Park
Wildlife SanctuaryLess strict than a national park; some controlled human activities permitted
Biosphere ReserveLarge protected area, consisting of a core (full protection), buffer, and transition zones; emphasis on human-nature coexistence
Sacred GrovesForest tracts protected by community/religious belief; example: Orans of Rajasthan (see Chapter 13)
📌 महत्वपूर्ण

💡 Three-tier structure of biosphere reserves: A biosphere reserve consists of three zones—the central core zone (where minimal human interference and complete protection is permitted), the buffer zone (where limited, controlled scientific research and traditional activities are permitted), and the transition zone (where sustainable economic activities of local communities continue). India has 18 declared biosphere reserves, some of which are also included in the UNESCO World Network.

◆ Self-location protection ◆ National Park ◆ Wildlife Sanctuary ◆ Biosphere Reserve ◆ core-buffer-transition zone ◆ sacred grove

9 Methods of Conservation - Ex-Situ Conservation

Ex-situ conservation means protecting a species outside its natural habitat, under controlled/artificial conditions—it is especially used when a species' natural population has declined so much that in-situ conservation alone is not sufficient, or when the natural habitat itself is not secure.

Types of off-site protectionCharacteristics and examples
ZooConservation of animal species, breeding programs and public awareness; example: white tiger breeding at Nandankanan Zoological Park (Odisha)
Botanical GardenLive collection and conservation of plant species
Seed/Gene BankLong-term conservation of genetic material (see Chapter 10); examples: NBPGR, Svalbard Seed Vault
Tissue Culture BankLaboratory-based conservation and reproduction of tissues of rare plant species
CryopreservationLong-term preservation of seeds, sperm and embryos at extremely low temperatures (up to -196°C, in liquid nitrogen)

11 Conservation of natural resources – forests, water and soil

The fundamental basis of biodiversity conservation rests on the conservation of three major natural resources – forests, water and soil – because these resources are the foundation of the entire ecosystem.

Forest Conservation

India's National Forest Policy (1988) aims to bring at least 33% of the country's total geographical area under forest/tree cover—currently it is around 24–25%. Programs such as Social Forestry and Agroforestry promote tree plantations with the participation of local communities.

Water conservation

Rainwater harvesting, groundwater recharge, and efficient irrigation techniques (drip/sprinkler irrigation) are key to water conservation. Rajasthan's traditional water conservation systems (johads, stepwells, and tankas—see Chapter 13) are globally acclaimed in this field.

soil Conservation

Soil conservation measures include contour ploughing, terrace farming, preventing soil erosion through tree plantation, and organic farming (reduced chemical fertilizer/pesticide use) – all of which help maintain soil fertility and productivity in the long term.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण — Interdependence of resources

Forest, water, and soil conservation are deeply interconnected—tree roots hold soil together (preventing erosion), healthy soil better absorbs rainwater (recharges groundwater), and adequate water supports vegetation growth. Therefore, environmental conservation policies should consider these three from an integrated perspective, not in isolation.

◆ National Forest Policy 1988 ◆ Social Forestry ◆ rainwater harvesting ◆ groundwater recharge ◆ contour plowing ◆ terrace farming ◆ Holistic Resource Management

12 The concept of continuous development – ​​theory and history

The most famous and widely accepted definition of sustainable development is the 1987 Brundtland Report ("Our Common Future").Our Common Future) – ‘development that meets the needs of the present generation in a manner that does not compromise the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.’ This definition was presented by a United Nations commission chaired by the then Prime Minister of Norway, Gro Harlem Brundtland.

Three pillars of sustainable development

🌍 Environmental Sustainability💰 Economic Stability
◆ judicious use of natural resources ◆ Ecosystem Conservation ◆ Pollution and carbon emission control◆ Inclusive and sustainable economic growth ◆ Efficient and equitable use of resources ◆ Poverty eradication

The third pillar—social equity—ensures that the benefits of development reach all sections of society, especially marginalized communities, equitably. The balance between these three pillars—environment, economy, and society—is the hallmark of truly sustainable development.

📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 💡 Journey beyond the Rio Conference

The 1972 Stockholm Conference first linked environment and development; the 1987 Brundtland Report popularized the term "sustainable development"; the 1992 Rio Earth Summit produced concrete frameworks such as Agenda 21 and the Convention on Biological Diversity; in 2015, the United Nations transformed this concept into 17 concrete, measurable goals by adopting the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs, detailed in the next topic).

◆ continuous development ◆ Brundtland Report 1987 ◆ Gro Harlem Brundtland ◆ Environmental pillar ◆ Economic pillar ◆ social inclusion ◆ Stockholm Conference 1972

13 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) – India's Progress

In September 2015, the United Nations General Assembly adopted the 2030 Agenda for Sustainable Development, which centers on 17 Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) – successors to the 2000 Millennium Development Goals (MDGs), but more comprehensive and applicable to all countries (not just developing ones).

14 Biodiversity Conservation and Sustainable Development Efforts in Rajasthan

Despite its unique ecological challenges (see Chapter 13), Rajasthan is taking several notable initiatives in the field of biodiversity conservation and sustainable development, which reflect a balance between traditional knowledge and modern policy making.

State-level conservation policies

  • Rajasthan State Biodiversity Board: Constituted under the Biodiversity Act 2002 (see Chapter 13), it works for the conservation, sustainable use of biological resources in the state and preservation of traditional knowledge of local communities.
  • Biodiversity Management Committees (BMC): Formed at the Gram Panchayat level, which document local biological resources (such as medicinal plants, indigenous seed varieties) through the People's Biodiversity Register.
  • Dedicated Breeding Centre for Great Indian Bustard Conservation (Jaisalmer): An ambitious project to incubate eggs under controlled conditions and raise chicks after releasing them into the wild, a combined model of ex-situ and in-situ conservation.

Rajasthan's steps towards sustainable development

  • Leader in Renewable Energy: Bhadla Solar Park (Jodhpur) and Jaisalmer wind farms make Rajasthan one of the largest clean energy producing states in India – directly contributing to SDG-7 (clean energy) and SDG-13 (climate action).
  • Water conservation models: Traditional systems like johads and tankas (see Chapter 13) are Rajasthan's unique contributions towards SDG-6 (clean water and sanitation).
  • Community Forest Management: Conservation of orans and other sacred groves strengthens SDG-15 (Landscaped Life) with the active participation of local communities.
  • Ecotourism-based livelihoods: Employment generation for local communities around protected areas such as Ranthambore, Sariska and Keoladeo, establishing a positive link between conservation and economic development (SDG-1, SDG-8).
📌 महत्वपूर्ण — 💡 Overall message of Rajasthan

The story of Rajasthan—whether it's tiger reintroduction in Sariska, water conservation through johads, or the clean energy revolution through the Bhadla Solar Park—shows that even the most arid and ecologically challenging regions, if combined with traditional knowledge and modern science and strong policy will, can become a national model for both biodiversity conservation and sustainable development. This is the central message of this chapter—conservation and development are not contradictory, but can complement each other.
